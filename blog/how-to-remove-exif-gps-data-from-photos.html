How to Remove EXIF and GPS Data from Photos Before Sharing

calendar_today October 2026 • schedule 4 min read • verified_user 100% Client-Side Privacy
pin_drop

In This Privacy Guide

Photos remember where they were taken, and they tell anyone who receives the file. This guide shows how to check what a photo stores, how to stop future photos from being geotagged, how to strip location from photos you already have, and how to verify the clean copy before you share it.

Why Remove EXIF and GPS Data

A photo carries more than a picture. Its EXIF metadata can include the exact GPS coordinates where it was taken, the date and time, and the camera that took it. That is harmless for your own archive, but the moment you share the file, you share all of it. A photo taken at home reveals your address. A photo of your children at school reveals the school. A holiday photo reveals that you are away. Removing EXIF and GPS data before sharing is one of the simplest privacy habits you can build.

First, Check What Is Actually Stored

Do not guess. Open the photo in the free EXIF viewer and look at the GPS fields first, then the camera and date fields. The viewer shows you exactly what a stranger would see if you shared the file as it is. Many people are surprised: photos they thought were clean turn out to carry precise coordinates, while others carry nothing at all. The check takes seconds and tells you whether removal is even needed.

Stop Future Photos from Being Geotagged

Removal fixes old photos, but prevention is easier. If you do not want future photos to carry a location, switch camera location off. On iPhone, go to Settings, then Privacy and Security, then Location Services, then Camera, and set it to Never. On Android, open the camera settings and turn the location tags option off. Our mobile geotagging guide walks through both paths. Photos taken after that change simply have no GPS data to remove.

Remove Location from Photos You Already Have

For photos that already carry GPS data, use a tool that edits metadata on your own device. Most desktop photo apps offer a remove-metadata or export-without-location option, and dedicated EXIF tools let you strip GPS fields while keeping the image itself untouched. Two warnings matter here. First, avoid uploading private photos to unknown online removers: sending the file to a stranger's server to protect your privacy defeats the purpose. Prefer a local app or a browser tool that processes on your device. Second, screenshots do strip EXIF data, but they also reduce quality and change the image, so treat them as a last resort, not a method.

Verify the Clean Copy Before You Share

Removal is only done when it is verified. After you strip the metadata, open the cleaned copy in the EXIF viewer again and confirm it now reports no GPS metadata. Check the copy you are about to share, not the original: it is easy to clean one file and then accidentally post another. If the viewer still shows coordinates, the removal did not take, and you should repeat it with a different tool.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does removing EXIF data lower photo quality?

Removing metadata alone does not touch the image pixels, so quality stays the same. Quality only drops if you re-save at a lower setting or use a screenshot, which re-encodes the picture.

Does deleting a photo's location remove it everywhere?

No. It only cleans the copy you edit. Copies already shared or uploaded still carry whatever they carried, so check before you share, not after.

Can I remove just the GPS but keep the camera settings?

It depends on the tool. Many metadata editors let you remove GPS fields selectively while keeping camera, lens, and date information. The viewer shows you what remains after any removal.

Do social media apps strip EXIF automatically?

Many strip metadata when you upload, but you should not rely on it. Policies change, and some platforms keep more than others. Verify the exact file you share instead of trusting the platform.

Is it illegal to remove EXIF data from my own photos?

No. Removing metadata from photos you own is your choice. Problems only arise if metadata is altered to mislead others, for example faking where or when evidence was captured.
