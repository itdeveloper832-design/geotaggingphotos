How to Find Where a Photo Was Taken: Read a Photo's EXIF Location

calendar_today October 2026 • schedule 4 min read • verified_user 100% Client-Side Privacy
pin_drop

In This Guide

Somewhere inside many photos sits the exact place they were taken, stored as GPS coordinates. This guide shows how to read that location with the photo location finder, what the coordinates mean, what to do when no location is found, and how to check a photo before you share it.

What the Photo Location Finder Does

The photo location finder reads the location that is already stored inside a photo. Every photo can carry EXIF metadata, and the GPS section of that metadata holds the latitude and longitude where the picture was taken. Upload a photo to the finder and it shows you those coordinates and the spot on a map. That is all it does, and that is the point: it answers one question, where was this photo taken, from the file itself.

Note what the finder is not. It has no place-search box. You cannot type a city name and ask where a photo was taken; the answer has to come from the photo's own stored data. If the file has no GPS data, the finder says so plainly instead of guessing.

Find a Photo's Location in 4 Steps

Open the photo location finder in your browser and upload the photo. The tool reads the EXIF data on your device and displays the latitude and longitude it finds, together with a map showing the capture location. Copy the coordinates if you need them elsewhere. That is the whole workflow, and it takes seconds because nothing is uploaded to a server.

What the Coordinates Tell You

Coordinates are just two numbers, but they unlock the place. Latitude runs from -90 to 90 and longitude from -180 to 180, and together they point to one exact spot. Paste a pair like 30.1575, 71.5249 into any maps app and it drops you on that location, in this case Multan, Pakistan. The finder gives you the numbers, the map position, and in most cases a detected place name (neighbourhood, city, and region) resolved from the coordinates. For street-level detail, paste the same coordinates into any maps app.

When No Location Is Found

The finder will sometimes report that no GPS metadata was found, and that result is usually correct. Screenshots do not carry the original photo's EXIF data. Many messaging and social apps strip metadata when you send or upload a picture. Older scanned photos never had GPS data to begin with. In all of these cases the file simply has nothing to read, and no tool can recover a location that was never stored. If you know where the photo was taken, you can add the location yourself with the add geotag tool and then check it again.

Finder vs EXIF Viewer: Which One Do You Need?

The two tools overlap but serve different questions. The finder is location-focused: coordinates plus a map, nothing else. The EXIF viewer shows the full metadata picture, including camera make and model, lens, exposure settings, and date, alongside the GPS fields. If you only care about where a photo was taken, use the finder. If you want everything the file knows about itself, use the viewer. Our guide on how to check if a photo has GPS coordinates covers the viewer workflow in detail.

Privacy: Check Before You Share

The finder is also a privacy tool. Before you post a photo publicly, run it through the finder and see exactly what location it broadcasts. A picture taken at home, at a school, or at a workplace carries that address with it wherever the file goes. Checking takes seconds, runs entirely in your browser, and tells you whether the copy you are about to share is safe to post.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the finder work without the original photo file?

No. The finder reads location data from the photo file itself, so it needs the actual file with its EXIF data intact. A link, a thumbnail, or a description of the photo is not enough.

Can it find a location from a screenshot?

No. Screenshots do not carry the original photo's EXIF GPS data, so the finder correctly reports that no location was found.

Does the finder upload my photo to a server?

No. All reading happens in your browser on your own device. Your photo never leaves your computer or phone.

Does the finder show a place name or just coordinates?

Both. It shows the stored coordinates and, when the location resolves, a detected place name with a map pin. For example, a Multan photo resolves to its neighbourhood, city, and region. For street-level detail, paste the coordinates into any maps app.

Can it locate an old scanned photo?

Not unless location data was added to it later. Scanned photos have no GPS data from the original moment, but you can add coordinates afterwards with the add geotag tool.
